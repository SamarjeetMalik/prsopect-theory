\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A team of learning agents can fail even when the task has a good cooperative solution: instead of
learning complementary behavior, agents can settle into locally safe, low-cooperation behavior.
We call this \emph{coordination collapse};
in the MARL literature its endpoint is the \emph{lazy-agent}
policy~\citep{liu2023lazy,peng2023curo}, and the underlying private-versus-collective conflict is
the one studied in sequential social dilemmas~\citep{leibo2017multi}. A common design response is to give
each learner part of the team's reward. We study that choice through a local/team \emph{reward-weighting}
dial, $x_i(w)=(1-w)\,r_i^{\mathrm{local}}+w\,r^{\mathrm{global}}$, and summarize where collapse sets in by a
finite-budget behavioral crossing $\wcrit$: the weight at which a fitted collapse probability passes 50\%,
estimated from pre-specified evaluation labels with seed-paired uncertainty.

Reward-transform comparisons along this dial look as if they should be clean. Multiplying every reward by a
positive constant leaves every optimal policy unchanged, and with per-minibatch advantage normalization it
leaves the policy-gradient step of PPO~\citep{schulman2017ppo} approximately unchanged as well. A symmetric
rescaling should therefore not move the crossing, whereas a transform that weights losses more than gains
changes which outcomes the learner prefers and could move it. Practical actor--critic implementations break
this separation in at least two ways, both because the critic's regression targets are usually not normalized.
First, at a fixed learning rate and budget the critic must fit returns that grow with the reward, so the quality of
its fit, from which the actor's advantages are built, can change. Second, the critic's gradient grows with the
reward, and Stable-Baselines3 PPO~\citep{raffin2021sb3}, like CleanRL's~\citep{huang2022cleanrl}, by default clips
the norm of the \emph{joint} actor--critic gradient; when the critic's gradient dominates that norm, the clip shrinks
the actor's step in proportion to the reward scale. Either way, a change in reward magnitude can change what is
learned even though it does not change what is optimal.

It is known that PPO's results depend on implementation details~\citep{engstrom2020implementation,andrychowicz2021matters,henderson2018matters},
that value-target scale can be normalized away~\citep{vanhasselt2016popart}, and that cooperative PPO
implementations normalize value targets~\citep{yu2022surprising}. What has not been shown is that an
unnormalized critic changes the \emph{conclusion} of a reward-design comparison, how large that change is on a
behavioral threshold measured with paired uncertainty, and which of the two routes carries it. We pre-registered
the second, the shared clip, as the mechanism; our experiments do not support it, and later pre-registered tests
locate the shift that remains without clipping in the scale of the critic's targets; that it acts through the critic's fit, the first route, is
consistent with the data but not isolated. We ask three questions.
\textbf{RQ1:} Does a symmetric reward rescaling move the finite-budget crossing under default PPO, and through
which route: the scale of the critic's targets or the shared gradient clip?
\textbf{RQ2:} Which interventions remove the shift, and does it survive decentralized execution?
\textbf{RQ3:} With rewards normalized and clipping removed, does a loss-only multiplier still move the crossing
relative to symmetric controls, including one matched to its reward scale?

The contributions, each tied to the evidence that supports it, are:
(i)~\emph{a reward-scale confound located in the critic's target scale} (Sections~\ref{sec:mechanism}
and~\ref{sec:optimizer}; Table~\ref{tab:hierarchy}). Under default SB3 PPO, symmetric $\times7$ scaling moves the
crossing by \VjtPStd{}, and a CleanRL-style implementation reproduces the shift (\VjtPElevenRaw{}, E11); intervals in
this list are calibrated jackknife-$t$ intervals. With normalized rewards the shift is not detectable in either
implementation (E1, E11), and normalizing only the critic's targets reduces it when three cohorts are pooled (forty
seeds, 42--81; change \VjtChPool{}), although none of those cohorts establishes this alone. On forty fresh seeds
without clipping (X4) the plain critic fits the scaled returns worse and its advantages are measurably worse, and two
critics that remove the target scale also remove the shift: a head whose output is fixed-scaled by the reward
multiplier, so that it regresses targets of the baseline's size (\VxfHead{}), and PopArt (\VxfPop{}); both are
equivalent to zero within $\pm0.10$. On forty more fresh seeds (X5) each node has its own actor network, acting on its
local observation, with a centralized critic, and the result repeats: the shift is \VxvPlain{} with the plain critic,
\VxvHead{} with the fixed-scale head and \VxvPop{} with PopArt. The confound therefore does not require a
parameter-shared, fully observed actor; what X5 keeps from the shared controller is a critic that sees the global
state and regresses unnormalized targets. Both interventions also equalize the critic's fit, so the fit is a candidate
mediator, not an isolated one.
(ii)~\emph{a pre-registered mechanism, measured and not supported} (Section~\ref{sec:optimizer} and
Appendix~\ref{app:clip}). We pre-registered the shared clip as the mechanism. Per-minibatch logging confirms the
distortion: the critic takes the shared clip, and under symmetric scaling the actor's applied gradient is
\VsixGradGlobal{} of the baseline's and its Adam step \VxoGlobStep{}. It is not shown to move the crossing. A per-group
clipping reduction in one ten-seed cohort (E6, \VjtChSix{}) did not replicate on twenty fresh seeds, where separate
clipping restored the actor's step and left the shift in place (X1, change \VxFCh{}). In an actor-by-critic clipping
factorial neither group's clip had a detectable effect, and the shift was \VxtNeither{} with no clipping at all (X3),
although X1's pre-registered no-clip shift was not detected (\VxFNone{}).
(iii)~\emph{a measurement protocol with checked uncertainty} (Sections~\ref{sec:design} and~\ref{sec:robustness}):
Firth logistic crossings with training-seed-level intervals, pre-registered experiments (\VfourTrajectories{} trajectories,
of which \VfourMechTrajectories{} address the optimizer), and a simulation study of interval procedures, whose model and assumptions Appendix~\ref{app:estimation} states, showing that the
pre-declared percentile intervals under-cover (\VcalPctCov{}) while a seed-level jackknife-$t$ interval is near
nominal (\VcalJtCov{}). Every principal verdict holds under the calibrated interval, but several between-condition
changes do not, and the fresh-seed replications that the calibration prompted did not support the mechanism we had
pre-registered.
(iv)~\emph{an asymmetric effect conditional on the normalization regime} (Section~\ref{sec:asymmetric}). With clipping removed and
rewards normalized, a loss-only multiplier raises the crossing relative to the symmetric control
(\VjtPAsymBoth{}), through 3.6M transitions, and under local observation (\VjtPTwelveAsym{}); in the second implementation,
with normalized rewards and its shared clip, A$-$S is \VjtPElevenAsym{}. Against a symmetric control whose training-reward mean and
SD track the multiplier arm's under its own policy, the effect remains under both critics, but its size depends
on the critic: with raw value targets it is small (\VjtPAMonPlain{}, which does not survive a Holm adjustment), because a symmetric reward with the same
moments reproduces most of the loss-only shift; with normalized value targets it is large (\VjtPAMonPop{}). In Level-Based Foraging an asymmetric transform also moves the
crossing, through a different incentive route (Section~\ref{sec:lbf}).

\emph{Scope.} On the supply chain the symmetric shift is established in two settings: a fully observed,
parameter-shared controller (Section~\ref{sec:method}), and per-node actors on local observations with a centralized
critic (X5). With a shared actor and critic both on local views and the shared clip (E12) it was not detectable. E12
differs from X5 in the learner, in the critic's input, in parameter sharing and in power (ten seeds against forty);
its interval includes X5's estimate, so it neither shows the shift absent nor tells us which difference matters. In the decentralized,
partially observed LBF environment, whose critic does not dominate the gradient, symmetric $\times4$ scaling moves the
crossing only slightly under the global clip (\VfourLBFSymGlobal{}) and not detectably without it (\VfourLBFSymSep{}).
Whether the critic-target confound reaches agents whose critics also see only local information is open.
We use ``boundary'' and ``crossing'' only in this operational, finite-budget sense, not as claims of
phase-like criticality.

Section~\ref{sec:related} positions the work; Section~\ref{sec:method} defines the reward, transforms,
label and estimand; Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} sets out the routes from reward scale to the policy and the
shared-clip pathway we pre-registered;
Section~\ref{sec:design} sets out the experiments and statistics; Sections~\ref{sec:optimizer}
and~\ref{sec:asymmetric} report symmetric and asymmetric transforms; Section~\ref{sec:lbf} turns to a second
environment; Section~\ref{sec:robustness} collects robustness checks; Sections~\ref{sec:limitations}
and~\ref{sec:discussion} state what the results do and do not establish.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Cooperative MARL, studied since independent $Q$-learning on shared
tasks~\citep{tan1993multi,busoniu2008comprehensive}, inherits the lazy-agent and
relative-overgeneralization failures in which agents abandon cooperation for a degenerate
policy~\citep{liu2023lazy,peng2023curo,tampuu2017multiagent}. The standard remedy is centralized
credit assignment under CTDE, a centralized critic or factorized value
function~\citep{lowe2017multi,yu2022surprising,sunehag2018vdn,rashid2018qmix,foerster2018counterfactual,son2019qtran};
related work ties the failure to decentralized exploration and parameter sharing, and locates an
empirical collapse threshold over payoff harshness for each exploration level, averaged over 30
seeds~\citep{weng2026}, or shows decentralized shaping cannot match centralized
baselines~\citep{dmarl2025}. This establishes \emph{that} centralization helps, \emph{how} to implement
it, and that collapse thresholds can be measured. It does not provide a 50\% collapse-probability
crossing along a local/team reward-weighting dial with seed-paired intervals, or test how reward
transforms and optimizer implementation move it. Combined
individual-and-team reward design has direct precedent: DE-MADDPG separates a per-agent and a team
critic for such mixtures~\citep{demaddpg2020}, packet-routing work studied the lazy-versus-selfish
trade-off with mixed and adaptive local/global signals~\citep{mao2020reward}, IRAT
learns individual- and team-reward policies with mutual discrepancy constraints~\citep{wang2022irat},
and CLOT balances individual and team optimization through a Lagrangian
multiplier~\citep{zhang2026clot}. These set or adapt the mixture as a design choice rather than
\emph{locating} a crossing with uncertainty.

Statistically, a 50\% crossing is an effective-dose or psychometric-threshold estimand. Dose--response
analysis routinely estimates ED50 values and differences between them~\citep{ritz2015dose}, and
psychometric work studies bootstrap uncertainty and grid placement~\citep{wichmann2001psychometric}.
We import that logic into trained multi-agent populations; our contribution lies in the experimental
unit, collapse label, seed-level inference, bracketing diagnostics and paired comparison of reward
designs. Treating collective learning as a dynamical system with critical points has precedent in
phase diagrams over environmental axes~\citep{yamaguchi2025}, metastable opinion-dynamics
boundaries~\citep{capannoli2026phase}, and mean-field limits of multi-agent
learning~\citep{hu2019meanfield,barfuss2019deterministic,hussain2025convergence}; ours is a
reward-structure threshold estimated from trained populations rather than asserted to be a dynamical
critical point.


PPO's behavior depends on implementation details that are not part of the algorithm's
definition~\citep{engstrom2020implementation,andrychowicz2021matters,henderson2018matters}, among them reward
or value normalization~\citep{vanhasselt2016popart} and gradient-norm clipping, which was introduced to bound
exploding gradients~\citep{pascanu2013difficulty}; cooperative PPO implementations normalize value targets by
default~\citep{yu2022surprising}. \citet{andrychowicz2021matters} vary, among many choices, whether actor and
critic share a network and how gradients are clipped, and measure returns. We hold the algorithm fixed and ask a
narrower question: whether one of these defaults, a critic without value normalization, changes the answer to a
reward-design question, and whether a second, a global clip shared by actor and critic, is what carries the change.
Closest to our setting, TOPPO~\citep{li2026toppo} traces PPO's
multi-task failures to ill-conditioned critic gradients and adds per-task value normalization (PopArt), a critic
layer normalization and a critic-gradient aggregation rule; its update also rescales actor and critic directions by
one joint norm clip. Its concern is the balance of critic learning across tasks. Ours is a reward-design comparison
measured on a behavioral threshold: a critic whose targets grow with reward scale does take the joint clip from the
actor, but restoring the actor's step does not remove the change in the comparison, whereas normalizing the rewards leaves no detectable change. We do not claim to be first to identify
critic-side gradient conditioning as a problem in PPO.

Human preference structure has entered collective learning only narrowly.
\citet{hughes2018inequity} show inequity aversion improves cooperation within the cooperative-AI
agenda~\citep{dafoe2020open,hammond2025multiagent,barfuss2020caring}; KTO aligns language models with a
Kahneman--Tversky value function carrying an explicit loss-aversion
coefficient~\citep{ethayarajh2024kto}; risk-sensitive RL optimizes tail
outcomes~\citep{qiu2021rmix,xie2023riskq,prashanth2014cvar,tamar2015optimizing}; and prospect theory has
entered single-agent RL with known calibration
concerns~\citep{prashanth2016cumulative,rabin2000risk,tversky1992}. \citet{mengesha2025} find loss
aversion non-monotone in an evolutionary quantal-response model, and \citet{lalmohammed2025} bring CPT
into cooperative MARL inside the actor--critic update, varying value and probability-weighting
components jointly with the optimizer. Unlike inequity aversion, which penalizes the \emph{difference}
between own and peer payoffs, our transform acts on the already-blended scalar against a fixed
reference, so it changes the magnitude and sign-weighting of the training signal rather than the
local-versus-collective trade-off itself.

\section{Problem Formulation and the Finite-Budget Crossing}
\label{sec:method}

We study \emph{reward-mixed} cooperative MARL, in which each learner's scalar reward is the blend
$x_i(w)=(1-w)\,r_i^{\mathrm{local}}+w\,r^{\mathrm{global}}$, $w\in[0,\allowbreak 1]$. On the supply
chain the team reward sums the five trained nodes' profits, so
$x_i(w)=r_i^{\mathrm{local}}+w\sum_{j\neq i}r_j^{\mathrm{local}}$: node $i$'s own profit keeps unit
weight and $w$ adds teammates' profits. We do not rescale $r_i^{\mathrm{local}}$ or
$r^{\mathrm{global}}$ at each $w$, so a given $w$ means the same mixture in every condition; the scale of
$x_i$ therefore varies with $w$, which matters for the critic-target and clipping effects studied in
Section~\ref{sec:optimizer}. Each experiment varies one factor at a time. The primary substrate is
\texttt{NetworkManagement-v1}~\citep{hubbs2020orgym}, a six-node multi-echelon inventory
network~\citep{oroojlooyjadid2022,gijsbrechts2022,marlim2023} wrapped with
PettingZoo/SuperSuit~\citep{terry2021pettingzoo,terry2020supersuit}; the second environment is
Level-Based Foraging (Section~\ref{sec:lbf}).

The boundary estimate follows four steps: fix the environment, learner, horizon, and collapse
label; train matched policies across a grid of $w$; label each run from pre-specified
\emph{evaluation} behavior, not training reward; and fit a monotone binary model
$P(\mathrm{collapse}\mid w)$, reporting the midpoint $\wcrit$ with seed-level confidence intervals.

\paragraph{Information structure.}
Except in E12 and X5, the supply-chain experiments train one policy, shared by the five trainable nodes, on each node's own
blended reward. Every node's policy input is the \emph{same} full observation of the network state; there is
no agent identifier, and actions are padded to the largest per-node action dimension, each node using its
leading entries. Under deterministic evaluation the five nodes therefore receive the same action vector. The
setting is thus a fully observed, homogeneous shared controller trained on per-node mixed rewards, not a
decentralized partially observed team: collapse here is the shared policy settling on the lazy action, and
nothing below except E12 and X5 bears on decentralized information. In Level-Based Foraging each agent's actor sees
only its own observation and a centralized critic sees the global state (Section~\ref{sec:lbf}).

\paragraph{Reward transforms.}
With zero as the gain/loss reference, $v(x)=x^\alpha$ for $x\ge0$ and $-\lambda(-x)^\beta$ for $x<0$.
The exponents $\alpha,\beta\in(0,\allowbreak 1]$ control \emph{curvature}; the multiplier $\lambda>1$
scales the loss branch. For $a>0$, $|v(-a)|/v(a)=\lambda a^{\beta-\alpha}$, which equals $\lambda$ at
every magnitude only when $\alpha=\beta$, as in all our experiments. The transform is applied after
blending,
\begin{equation}
 r_i^{\PT}=v\!\left((1-w)\,r_i^{\mathrm{local}}+w\,r^{\mathrm{global}}\right).
 \label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
Controls use $r_i^{\PT}=v((x_i-z_0)/s_{\mathrm{in}})/s_{\mathrm{out}}$ with no addition of $z_0$ after
transformation; Equation~\ref{eq:reward} has $z_0=0$ and $s_{\mathrm{in}}=s_{\mathrm{out}}=1$. The scale
controls of Section~\ref{sec:optimizer} use the frozen empirical-mean reference $z_0=-2.6281398333$
(Appendix~\ref{app:configuration}) with $u=x-z_0$ and $\phi(u)=\mathrm{sign}(u)|u|^{.88}$: arm
B$=\phi(u)$ (curvature only), A$=v_7(u)$ (losses $\times7$), S$=7\phi(u)$ (both branches $\times7$,
i.e.\ $s_{\mathrm{out}}=1/7$), and R$=3.451\,\phi(u)$ (both branches scaled by A's pooled RMS ratio).
We omit the probability-weighting channel of full CPT. Unlike \citet{lalmohammed2025}, the transform
sits outside the optimizer, so within a fixed optimizer configuration matched runs differ only in the
scalar training signal.

\paragraph{Collapse label.}
A \emph{collapse label} is a pre-specified binary evaluation rule scored on held-out evaluation
episodes. In the supply chain, collapse is $\mathbf{1}[\bar S<10\wedge\bar\pi\le-127.1]$, where
$\bar\pi$ is the total profit of nodes 2--6 averaged over ten deterministic evaluation episodes
(seeds $10s,\dots,10s+9$ for training seed $s$) and $\bar S$ is the mean over the five trained nodes of
the deterministic base-stock target $250(a_1+1)$ chosen at the reset of the first evaluation episode,
where $a_1$ is the node's first action component. Node 1 is scripted to order 20 units, split equally
across its action entries. Collapsed runs sit at the lazy-policy profit floor ($\approx-128$), so the
profit clause is a redundancy check on $\bar S<10$; threshold variants of $\pm20\%$ change $\wcrit$ by
at most 0.015. Across all \VrevLabelN{} E1 runs only \VrevLabelNearS{} has $\bar S$ within one unit of the
threshold, so the label is not a fine cut through a continuum (Figure~\ref{fig:label}, Appendix~\ref{app:factorial}).

\paragraph{Estimand.}
We write the estimand as $w_{50}(T;E,A,V,U,L,Q)$: training budget, environment, learner (including its
optimizer configuration), transform, reward units, collapse label and evaluation protocol. It is a
fitted 50\% crossing, not a safe operating point, and a rightward shift means more team weight is needed
to reach it. We fit $P(\mathrm{collapse}\mid w)=\sigma(b_0+b_1w)$ and report $\wcrit=-b_0/b_1$
(Section~\ref{sec:design}). Collapse becomes less likely as $w$ grows, so $b_1<0$ in every fit we report.

\section{How Reward Scale Can Reach the Policy in PPO}
\label{sec:mechanism}

\paragraph{Learner and optimizer.}
The supply-chain learner is parameter-shared PPO in Stable-Baselines3 2.8.0~\citep{raffin2021sb3}:
one policy across nodes, separate actor and critic MLPs, a single Adam optimizer ($\epsilon=10^{-5}$,
SB3's implicit default) over both, advantages normalized per minibatch, no value normalization, and one
global gradient-norm clip of 0.5 applied to the concatenated actor and critic gradient. Because the
actor's advantages are normalized but the critic's targets are not, a larger reward scale enlarges only
the value-loss gradient, which then dominates the shared clip. Section~\ref{sec:optimizer} varies
exactly this coupling. Training budgets count requested agent transitions; with five trained lanes and
128-step rollouts each rollout adds 640 transitions, so 300k requested transitions execute 300,160
(Appendix~\ref{app:configuration}).

\paragraph{How reward scale reaches the actor.}
Write the PPO loss as $\mathcal L=\mathcal L_\pi(\theta_a)+c_v\,\mathcal L_V(\theta_c)$ with value-loss coefficient
$c_v=0.5$ and no entropy term, where the actor parameters $\theta_a$ and critic parameters $\theta_c$ are
disjoint (the networks share no trainable parameter). On a minibatch let $g_a=\nabla_{\theta_a}\mathcal L$
and $g_c=\nabla_{\theta_c}\mathcal L$. SB3 rescales the concatenated gradient to norm at most $\tau=0.5$,
\begin{equation}
\tilde g=\kappa\,(g_a,g_c),\qquad \kappa=\min\!\Bigl(1,\;\frac{\tau}{\sqrt{\|g_a\|^2+\|g_c\|^2}}\Bigr),
\qquad\text{so}\qquad \|\tilde g_a\|=\kappa\|g_a\|\approx\tau\,\frac{\|g_a\|}{\|g_c\|}\ \ \text{when}\ \|g_c\|\gg\|g_a\|.
\label{eq:clip}
\end{equation}
Multiply every reward by $s>0$. Returns, value targets and, at a fixed relative fit, the value error scale by
$s$, so $\|g_c\|=c_v\|\nabla_{\theta_c}\tfrac12(V-\hat R)^2\|$ scales by about $s$; the advantages also scale by
$s$, but are normalized per minibatch, so $\|g_a\|$ does not. By Equation~\ref{eq:clip} the applied actor
gradient then falls by about $1/s$. Clipping actor and critic \emph{separately} replaces $\kappa$ with
$\kappa_a=\min(1,\tau/\|g_a\|)$, which does not involve the critic, and normalizing rewards or value targets
keeps $\|g_c\|$ from growing with $s$. Adam~\citep{kingma2015adam} divides each coordinate by a running
root-mean-square of its own gradient, so it would undo a \emph{constant} rescaling of $\tilde g_a$. It does not
fully undo this one, because $\kappa$ varies from minibatch to minibatch with the critic's error and because
the denominator is floored at $\epsilon$. The applied gradient is therefore not the quantity that reaches the
policy; the parameter update is, and it is what E6 measures directly. We do not isolate how much of the
remaining attenuation is due to the varying $\kappa$ and how much to $\epsilon$.

The argument makes four testable predictions, which Section~\ref{sec:optimizer} and Appendix~\ref{app:clip} check:
(a)~under the shared clip the critic's pre-clip norm grows with the reward multiplier while the actor's does
not; (b)~the actor's applied gradient, and to a lesser degree its parameter update, shrink under symmetric
scaling; (c)~per-group clipping restores the actor's update and removes the part of the crossing shift that
runs through $\kappa$; (d)~what remains after (c) runs through the critic's targets, so normalizing rewards
or targets should remove it. Predictions (a) and (b), and the optimizer half of (c), are confirmed (Section~\ref{sec:optimizer}, Appendix~\ref{app:clip}). The
behavioural half of (c) is not: restoring the actor's update reduced the crossing shift in one cohort (E6) but not
on fresh seeds (X1), and when actor and critic are clipped independently neither clip has a detectable effect on the
shift (X3). For (d), no symmetric shift is detectable under reward normalization with or without the shared clip (E1),
and critic-only target normalization reduces the shift on the pooled cohorts and, on forty fresh seeds, removes it (X4), also under decentralized execution (X5). As a quantitative check of (b), we evaluated Equation~\ref{eq:clip} on every logged minibatch of the default
cell. The critic accounts for more than 99.99\% of the squared joint norm in both arms, every minibatch is clipped,
and the predicted S/B ratio of applied actor gradients, \VeqPred{}, equals the measured \VeqMeas{} (largest
per-minibatch relative error $\VeqMaxErr{}$); under per-group clipping both are \VeqGroupMeas{}. Because
Equation~\ref{eq:clip} is how the clip is computed, this check does not test the clip itself. What it establishes
is that the clip acts on the joint norm, that $\kappa\approx\tau/\|g_c\|$ throughout, and that the actor's
attenuation is fully accounted for by the critic's gradient. Evaluating the formula at cell-mean norms instead gives
\VrvPredRatio{}, because those norms are heavy-tailed. Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism} summarizes the pathway and
where each intervention acts.

\paragraph{The other route: the critic's fit.}
The clip pathway is thus measured link by link, but the crossing shift is not shown to depend on it, which leaves the first route
of Section~\ref{sec:intro}. With raw targets, a critic trained at a fixed learning rate must move its output to returns
that are seven times larger, and at a fixed budget it fits them worse; the actor's advantages are normalized per
minibatch, but they are computed from that fit, so their relative error can grow with the reward scale. We
pre-registered this route first only as the explanation of the no-clip residual (E7, E8, E10); that it carries the shift
without the clip's help is a reading we reached after X1--X3. The evidence for it was then indirect: the critic's explained variance is lower under
symmetric scaling in every clipping mode (E1, X3), and normalizing the rewards, or only the critic's targets, leaves no
detectable shift or, on the pooled cohorts, reduces it (E1, E7, E8, E10, E11). Without clipping, the learner's definition
leaves the critic's regression targets as the only route by which a symmetric reward scale reaches training, apart from
Adam's $\epsilon$: there is no entropy term, the value loss is unclipped, advantages are normalized per minibatch, the
networks share no parameter, and Adam's update is otherwise invariant to a constant rescaling of a parameter's gradient. A pre-registered test on fresh seeds (X4, Section~\ref{sec:optimizer}) therefore
removes the target scale while keeping everything else, and separately equalizes the critic's fit.

\paragraph{Experiments.}
Table~\ref{tab:matrix} lists the seventeen pre-registered experiments. Together they comprise \VfourTrajectories{}
trajectories; the \VfourMechTrajectories{} in E1, E2, E6--E11 and X1--X5 address the optimizer mechanism (E11's 720 and
X2's 640 in a second PPO implementation), E3 and E5 follow 540 trajectories to 3.6M transitions, E4's 800 run on the second environment, and
E12's 480 change the information structure (as do X5's 1,920, counted above). Earlier ``legacy'' sweeps from another platform
(20 seeds per cell) supply the curvature-versus-multiplier decomposition and the cross-sectional horizon
fit; they are reported separately and never pooled with E1--E12 or X1--X5.

\paragraph{Estimation.}
We fit the logistic curve by Firth's bias-reduced maximum likelihood~\citep{firth1993}, which stays finite
under the complete separation that ten-seed cells often show. Every interval is computed over complete training
seeds and paired across the conditions of an experiment. The reported interval is the seed-level jackknife-$t$
interval of Section~\ref{sec:stats}; the pre-declared interval, from 2,000 multinomial draws of complete training-seed
blocks~\citep{efron1993bootstrap}, is kept as a sensitivity analysis. Out-of-grid and non-converged fits are retained
and counted, never clipped. Logistic, probit and decreasing isotonic fits on the same rows serve as sensitivity
estimators (convention in Appendix~\ref{app:estimation}); their maximum spread on the supply-chain data
is 0.018.

\paragraph{Interval calibration.}
\label{sec:stats}
The training seed is the independent unit: every interval resamples or leaves out complete seed blocks, never
checkpoints or evaluation episodes. The pre-declared intervals are seed-block percentile bootstraps, and with ten
seeds these are known to be narrow. We therefore simulated the design, added in revision and not pre-registered
(Appendix~\ref{app:estimation}): ten, twenty or thirty seeds, the E1 grid and one label per seed and cell, across
\VcalScen{} data-generating scenarios with 10,000 replicates each. The scenarios vary the curve's steepness and link,
per-seed random intercepts and slopes, how strongly seed effects are shared between paired arms, arm-specific
heterogeneity, the size of the contrast, and crossings near either edge of the grid. Five interval procedures were
compared. At ten seeds the pre-declared percentile intervals covered the true contrast in \VcalPctCov{} of
replicates and excluded zero under a null contrast in \VcalPctFP{} of them, rather than 5\%; their nominal 99\%
version covered in only \VcalPctCovNine{}, so it is not a calibrated interval, and a BCa correction is no better
(\VcalBcaCov{}). A hierarchical logistic model and a parametric bootstrap from it covered well when the model held but
not when seed effects were not shared across arms (false-positive rates up to \VcalGlmmFPMax{} and \VcalPbootFPMax{}).
One procedure stayed near nominal in every scenario: a seed-level jackknife-$t$ interval, the estimate $\pm
t_{n-1}$ times the leave-one-seed-out standard error, which covered the contrast in \VcalJtCov{} and the single
crossing in \VcalJtCovW{} of replicates, with a null false-positive rate of \VcalJtFP{}. It is about
\VcalWidthRatio{} times as wide; its power for a 0.1 contrast on a steep curve is \VcalPowTen{} at ten seeds,
\VcalPowTwenty{} at twenty and \VcalPowThirty{} at thirty. We report the jackknife-$t$ interval as the calibrated
inference throughout and keep the pre-declared percentile interval beside it (Table~\ref{tab:results}). For all
\VrvNContrasts{} principal contrasts the two give the same zero-exclusion verdict at 95\%. A Holm adjustment of the
jackknife-$t$ $p$-values across the \VrvNContrasts{} contrasts keeps \VjtHolmKept{} of the \VjtNExcl{} nonzero
contrasts; three do not survive it (\VjtHolmLost{}), and we say so where they are discussed. The calibration matters
most for \emph{changes} in a contrast between conditions, which we report with jackknife-$t$ intervals over the
seeds the two conditions share: several changes that excluded zero under the percentile interval do not under the
calibrated one (Sections~\ref{sec:optimizer} and~\ref{sec:limitations}). An independent set of 10,000 bootstrap draws
moves no percentile endpoint by more than \VrvMCShift{}. Intervals are pointwise; we make no simultaneous claim beyond
the stated Holm family. Every interval in the text, tables and figures of E1--E12 and X1--X5 is the 95\% jackknife-$t$ interval unless it is
labelled as the pre-declared percentile interval (Wilson intervals on observed proportions and $\pm1.96$ standard-error
bars on reward moments are labelled where they appear); the legacy sweeps of Sections~\ref{sec:asymmetric} and~\ref{sec:robustness}
keep their seed-block intervals. Table~\ref{tab:results} gives both intervals for every principal contrast, and
Table~\ref{tab:pct_sensitivity} (Appendix~\ref{app:estimation}) lists the \VpctSensN{} reported intervals whose zero-exclusion
verdict differs between them; in each, the percentile interval excludes zero and the jackknife-$t$ interval does not.
Where a pre-declared prediction was judged with the percentile interval and the verdict differs, we say so. An interval that includes zero is reported as no detectable effect, never as evidence of no
effect; we make equivalence statements only against a stated margin.

\paragraph{Pre-registration and platform.}
Experiments E1--E12 and X1--X5 were pre-declared, with source and design hashes frozen before launch; the
departures (one pre-execution source fix, a label-threshold fallback corrected from stored data, and
analysis-code fixes) are logged in Appendix~\ref{app:prereg}. All E1--E12 and X1--X5 trajectories run on one CPU
platform. An earlier platform does not reproduce trained policies
bit-for-bit, but collapse labels agree in \VfourAgreeControl{} of matched direct-control runs and
\VfourAgreeHorizon{} of matched horizon checkpoints, so we make reproducibility claims at the level of
labels and crossings. The legacy sweeps in Sections~\ref{sec:asymmetric} and~\ref{sec:robustness} (20 seeds
per cell) come from the earlier platform and are not pooled with E1--E12 or X1--X5.

\section{Symmetric Reward Scaling and the Critic's Target Scale}
\label{sec:optimizer}

Table~\ref{tab:results} and Figure~\ref{fig:summary} collect the principal contrasts of this and the next two
sections, and Table~\ref{tab:hierarchy} the evidence for the symmetric shift, level by level. This section establishes
the shift under the default learner (E1, E2). With normalized rewards it is not detectable (E1), and normalizing only
the critic's targets reduces it when three cohorts are pooled (E7, E8, E10). Without clipping a smaller shift remains,
and pre-registered tests on fresh seeds locate that shift in the scale of the critic's regression targets, with a
shared controller (X4) and with decentralized execution (X5). The shared-clip pathway that we had
pre-registered as the mechanism is summarized after that: it is measured, but not shown to move the crossing (details
in Appendix~\ref{app:clip}). The section closes with the second implementation (E11) and the optimizer dependence of
the baseline. The quantities involved are distinct: the \emph{pre-clip} gradient norm, the \emph{applied} gradient
after clipping, the parameter \emph{update} after Adam, and the \emph{crossing} estimated from evaluation behavior.

\paragraph{Design (E1, E2).}
The pre-registered factorial crosses two optimizer modes with two reward modes at 300k. The optimizer
modes are SB3's default global clip and separate actor and critic Adam optimizers without clipping. The
reward modes are raw transformed rewards and online normalization (\texttt{VecNormalize} of rewards by
the running standard deviation of discounted returns, no observation normalization). Each cell runs the
four arms B, A, S and R on eight mixture cells $\{0,.2,.4,.5,.6,.7,.85,1\}$ with seeds 42--51, for
1,280 trajectories. An addendum (E2, 320 trajectories) keeps the default clip and raw rewards but sets
Adam's $\epsilon$ to $10^{-8}$. The pre-declared readings were: an A$-$S interval excluding zero after
both interventions indicates a sign-asymmetric effect; collapse of the symmetric contrasts after an
intervention indicates a scale or optimizer mechanism.

\paragraph{Under the default learner, symmetric scaling mimics loss asymmetry.}
In the default cell every minibatch exceeds the clip norm, and the critic's pre-clip gradient norm
grows from \VfourCriticNormB{} (B) to \VfourCriticNormS{} (S) while the actor's stays near 2. The applied
actor update after clipping is \VfourAppliedActorB{} for B and \VfourAppliedActorS{} for S
(Table~\ref{tab:factorial_diagnostics}, Appendix~\ref{app:factorial}). Symmetric $\times7$ scaling moves the crossing by
\VfourSymStd{} relative to B, about as far as the loss multiplier: A$-$S is
\VfourAsymStd{} (Table~\ref{tab:factorial_contrasts}, Appendix~\ref{app:clip}).

\paragraph{With normalized rewards no symmetric shift is detectable; without the clip a shift remains.}
With normalized rewards the critic's gradient falls below the clip norm, the actor carries almost all
of the gradient energy, and no symmetric shift is detectable (S$-$B \VfourSymNorm{}). With separate
optimizers alone the symmetric $\times7$ effect is \VfourSymSep{}, and the $\times3.451$ effect is
no longer detectable. The change in the $\times7$ effect from the default cell, \VfourSymSepChange{}, does not exclude
zero, although its pre-declared percentile interval did (\VfourSymSepChangePct{}). With both interventions, S$-$B is \VfourSymBoth{}. The normalized and fully decoupled intervals include zero, but
they are not equivalence results: under both interventions the interval extends beyond a $\pm.05$ margin, so
a symmetric shift of that size is not excluded. The changes in each contrast relative to the default cell are
in Table~\ref{tab:factorial_interactions}; Figure~\ref{fig:factorial} summarizes them.

\paragraph{The artifact is not Adam's $\epsilon$, and points to critic target magnitude.}
Lowering $\epsilon$ to $10^{-8}$ leaves the symmetric effect in place (S$-$B \VfourSymEps{}; change
relative to default \VfourSymEpsChange{}), contrary to our pre-declared alternative that the clipped
actor gradients were being suppressed by Adam's $\epsilon$ floor. Normalizing rewards, which bounds the
critic's targets, removes the detectable effect, whereas without the clip a shift remains. The
critic's explained variance agrees: without clipping and with raw rewards it falls from
\VfourEVSepRawB{} (B) to \VfourEVSepRawS{} (S), whereas under reward normalization it is \VfourEVNormB{}
and \VfourEVNormS{}. Two qualifications limit this account. First, \texttt{VecNormalize} normalizes
rewards, not value targets as PopArt does~\citep{vanhasselt2016popart}; E7 and E8 below normalize the targets
alone. Second, the separate-optimizer
arm removes clipping as well; because Adam is per-parameter, splitting one Adam into two changes nothing
by itself, so this arm tests the removal of clipping, not optimizer grouping.

\paragraph{Normalizing only the critic's targets (E7, E8, E10).}
Reward normalization rescales both the critic's targets and the rewards from which the actor's advantages
are built. E7 keeps separate optimizers without clipping and raw rewards, and crosses two critics with four
arms on the E1 grid (400 new runs; the plain-critic B, A and S runs are E1's, licensed by a gate that
reproduced an E1 run bit-for-bit). The \emph{plain} critic regresses raw returns. The \emph{PopArt} critic
reparametrizes its output as $V(x)=\sigma\,h(x)+\mu$, with $\mu,\sigma$ running moments of the rollout returns
and an output-preserving rescale of the head whenever they change~\citep{vanhasselt2016popart}; the actor's
rewards, advantages and loss are untouched. PopArt repairs the critic's fit: explained variance is
\VsevenEVPopB{} (B) and \VsevenEVPopS{} (S), against \VsevenEVPlainB{} and \VsevenEVPlainS{} with the plain
critic. The symmetric shift falls from \VsevenSymPlain{} to \VsevenSymPop{}, but the change, \VsevenSymChange{},
does not exclude zero. Our pre-declared criterion for attributing the residual to critic target scale required
both, so this reading is inconclusive: the point estimate is a partial reduction, and ten seeds cannot
separate it from none (Table~\ref{tab:e7}, Appendix~\ref{app:clip}). Two fresh cohorts pre-registered after E7 repeated the comparison: E8
(seeds 52--61, Table~\ref{tab:e8}) and E10 (twenty seeds, 62--81, 640 runs). In both, the plain critic keeps a
symmetric shift (\VeightBSymPlain{}; \VtenSymPlain{}) and PopArt leaves none detectable (\VeightBSymPop{};
\VtenSymPop{}). The change met the pre-declared criterion under the pre-declared percentile intervals in each cohort
(\VeightBChangePct{}; \VtenChangePct{}), but under the calibrated jackknife-$t$ interval neither cohort alone excludes zero
(\VjtChEight{}; \VjtChTen{}). Pooling the three cohorts, forty seeds in all, a secondary analysis declared in advance
for E10, gives \VjtChPool{}, which excludes zero also at 99\% (\VjtChPoolNine{}). We therefore attribute the residual
of prediction (d), the shift that remains without clipping, to critic target scale on the pooled evidence, not on any
single cohort; X4, below, tests it on forty fresh seeds.

\paragraph{The critic's target scale carries the shift (X4).}
X4 (1,920 runs, forty fresh seeds 122--161) tests this account directly, with separate actor and critic Adam, no
clipping and raw rewards; it was pre-registered with the jackknife-$t$ interval as primary and gated on bit-for-bit
reproduction of an E1 and an E7 run (Appendix~\ref{app:prereg}). It compares three critics. The plain critic and the
PopArt critic are E7's. The third, a \emph{fixed-scale head}, multiplies the plain critic's output by the arm's reward
scale, $V(x)=c\,h(x)$ with $c=1$ for B and $c=7$ for S, so that the network $h$ regresses targets of B's size. By the
invariance argument of Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}, S then trains as B does apart from Adam's $\epsilon$; with $c=1$ the
head reproduces the plain critic bit-for-bit. At every rollout, a separate reference critic, fitted out of sample to
Monte-Carlo returns, measures the learner's value fit and the quality of its advantages; this logging leaves training
bit-for-bit unchanged (verified on the gate run). With the plain critic the symmetric shift replicates (\VxfPlain{}), the critic fits the scaled
returns worse (S$-$B in explained variance against Monte-Carlo returns \VxfFitPlain{}), and the actor's
advantages are worse: relative to B, value error is \VxfVerr{} times, advantage error \VxfGae{} times and sign
disagreement \VxfSign{} times as large, and rank agreement differs by \VxfRank{} (Table~\ref{tab:x4q}, Appendix~\ref{app:clip}; each of the four
95\% intervals excludes equality). The fixed-scale head equalizes the fit (gap \VxfFitHead{}, 90\% interval
\VxfFitHeadNinety{}, within the declared $\pm0.05$) and removes the shift: S$-$B is \VxfHead{}, its 90\% interval
\VxfHeadNinety{} lies within $\pm0.10$, and the change from the plain critic is \VxfChHead{} (Table~\ref{tab:x4}). PopArt also equalizes the fit (\VxfFitPop{}, \VxfFitPopNinety{}) and the advantage measures (every S/B ratio within \VxfQualMax{} of one, as under the head), and leaves S$-$B at \VxfPop{}, equivalent to zero within $\pm0.10$ (\VxfPopNinety{});
the change from the plain critic, \VxfChPop{}, excludes zero in this one cohort under the jackknife-$t$ interval, which none of E7, E8 and E10 did alone.
All six pre-declared predictions are met. By the readings declared before the runs, the scale of the critic's
regression targets carries the symmetric shift at this budget, and the result is consistent with its acting through
the critic's fit. It does not show that the fit is the mediator: both interventions that equalize the fit also remove
the target scale, and PopArt changes the parametrization as well as the fit.

\paragraph{The same channel under decentralized execution (X5).}
X4, like every supply-chain experiment except E12, trains one actor that sees the full state. X5 (1,920 runs, forty
fresh seeds 162--201) repeats X4's three critics with centralized training and decentralized execution. Each of the
five trained nodes has its own actor network, action head and log-standard deviation. Each acts on its local view
(E12's: its own retail demand, inventory and incoming order pipeline) plus a one-hot identifier. One critic sees the
global state and the identifier, which it needs because each node's mixed reward differs. No actor parameter is shared.
The gate verified that a node's actor receives exactly zero gradient from other nodes' samples and that the critic
ignores the local-only inputs (Appendix~\ref{app:prereg}). The learner is X4's: separate Adam, no clipping, raw
rewards.

With the plain critic the shift is present (\VxvPlain{}), as large as under the shared controller in X4

and sign disagreement \VxvSign{} times as large, and rank agreement differs by \VxvRank{} (Table~\ref{tab:x5q}, Appendix~\ref{app:clip}; each of

fixed-scale head and PopArt each equalize the fit (gaps \VxvFitHead{} and \VxvFitPop{}, 90\% intervals
\VxvFitHeadNinety{} and \VxvFitPopNinety{}) and leave shifts of \VxvHead{} and \VxvPop{}. Both are equivalent to zero

All six pre-declared predictions are met. By the declared readings, the critic's target scale carries the shift under

result is for 300k transitions without clipping. A third is new. The critic here is centralized, so X5 does not test
agents whose critics see only local information. Nor does it explain why E12, with a shared actor and critic both on
local views and the shared clip, found no detectable shift.

\paragraph{The shared-clip pathway: measured, but not shown to move the crossing.}
We pre-registered the shared clip as the mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}); Appendix~\ref{app:clip} reports the
five experiments that tested it (E6, E9, X1--X3). The distortion is real. Under the shared clip S's applied actor
gradient is \VsixGradGlobal{} of B's, and after Adam its parameter update is \VsixUpdGlobal{} of B's (E6) and its Adam
step \VxoGlobStep{} (X1). It is not shown to move the crossing. In one ten-seed cohort, clipping actor and critic
separately reduced the shift (E6, change \VjtChSix{}), and as the clip threshold rose the point
estimates fell (E9, change \VjtChNineDose{}, which only the pre-declared percentile interval excludes from zero). On

cohorts, an exploratory analysis, gives \VxeNone{} without clipping and \VxeGroup{} with per-group clipping, so clipping
may enlarge the shift, by an amount on which the cohorts disagree. Clipping is therefore not necessary for the shift,
and is not shown to carry it; how much of the larger clipped shift runs through the critic's targets is not tested.

\paragraph{The confound is not specific to SB3 (E11).}

base.

the learner's optimizer configuration as well as of the reward design; comparisons are valid only
within a fixed configuration.

\section{Asymmetric Reward Transforms}
\label{sec:asymmetric}

Whether a loss-only multiplier changes what is learned beyond its effect on reward scale depends on the normalization
regime. Under the default learner its effect cannot be told apart from symmetric scaling (Section~\ref{sec:optimizer}).
With rewards normalized and no clipping it is positive against the symmetric control. With raw rewards and no
clipping, against a symmetric control matched online in mean and SD (E8), it is large when the critic's targets are
normalized and small when they are not. This section
reports these results in that order.

\paragraph{The loss multiplier opposes curvature.}
In the legacy decomposition at 300k (20 seeds per cell), the linear null gives \RepeatedNull{}; this is a
separate run from the structural sweep of Section~\ref{sec:robustness} (\Headline{}), with a different
$w$-grid, and the decomposition uses its own null. Curvature
alone ($\alpha{=}\beta{=}0.88$, the conventional prospect-theory estimate~\citep{tversky1992}, not tuned)
lowers the crossing to 0.585 ($-0.100$ $[-0.145,-0.057]$ vs.\ linear), and raising $\lambda$ from 1 to 7
raises it to \HighLambda{} (Figure~\ref{fig:decomposition}; per-$\lambda$ values in
Appendix~\ref{app:estimation}). Reported as a single ``prospect-theoretic'' treatment the two effects
partially cancel. Against the curvature baseline the loss-multiplier shift is \LossMultiplierShift{}.
On its own, this shift cannot distinguish loss asymmetry from the larger reward magnitude the multiplier
creates; Section~\ref{sec:optimizer} showed how; this section separates the two.

\paragraph{With clipping removed or rewards normalized, the loss multiplier still moves the crossing (E1, E6).}
Because A and S apply the same multiplier to losses and differ only in whether gains are also scaled, A$-$S
compares two gain-to-loss ratios (1:7 and 1:1) at matched loss scale, and A$-$B isolates the loss-branch
multiplier at fixed gains. A$-$S is positive and excludes zero under normalization (\VfourAsymNorm{}),
separate optimizers (\VfourAsymSep{}), both together (\VfourAsymBoth{}) and per-group clipping
(\VsixAsymGroup{}), and A$-$B is \VfourLossBoth{} with both interventions, so the pre-declared criterion for a
sign-asymmetric effect is met. In the second implementation with reward normalization A$-$S is
\VelevenAsymDefault{} (E11). How much of each reward falls on the loss branch depends on $w$ and on the
learned policy, because $z_0$ is fixed; near the crossings both branches are active
(Table~\ref{tab:loss_occupancy}). The arms also differ in reward mean, not only in scale: A's transformed
rewards have a negative mean at most $w$, whereas S and R have positive means that grow with $w$
(Figure~\ref{fig:reward_moments}, Appendix~\ref{app:factorial}). A$-$S therefore compares gain-to-loss ratios
but does not hold the first two moments fixed.

\paragraph{The asymmetric contrast persists with budget (E5).}
To test the asymmetric effect over budget without the scale pathway, we repeated the horizon design with
the fully decoupled learner of Section~\ref{sec:optimizer} (separate optimizers without clipping,
normalized rewards) and arms B, A and S: 270 trajectories on the same nine cells to 3.6M. All three
pre-declared predictions are met (Table~\ref{tab:e5}, Appendix~\ref{app:horizon}). A$-$S is positive at every primary budget, from
\VfiveAsymStart{} at 300k to \VfiveAsymEnd{} at 3.6M, and no symmetric shift is detectable at any budget
(S$-$B \VfiveSymStart{} at 300k, \VfiveSymEnd{} at 3.6M). The loss-only contrast A$-$B is positive to
900k and not detectable afterwards. Under this learner the crossings fall to 900k and then rise as many
initially coordinated trajectories relapse into collapse, and no arm stabilizes. The asymmetric effect
therefore persists over the tested budgets while the absolute crossings keep moving.
Figure~\ref{fig:e5} shows all 24 checkpoints. This reading of the non-primary checkpoints is exploratory
and its intervals are pointwise. The A$-$S interval excludes zero at \VrevAsymPos{} checkpoints, with
point estimates between \VrevAsymMin{} and \VrevAsymMax{}. The S$-$B interval excludes zero at
\VrevSymNeg{} intermediate checkpoints (\VrevSymNegSpan{}), all with S \emph{below} B, and at no
checkpoint with S above B. The default-optimizer E3 contrast is drawn for comparison; its own crossings
are in Figure~\ref{fig:horizon} (Appendix~\ref{app:horizon}).

\paragraph{Against a scale-matched symmetric control, the effect depends on the critic (E7).}
E7's arm M is an increasing affine map $a_w r_{\mathrm{B}}+b_w$ of B's reward, symmetric in gains and losses,
with constants frozen so that M has A's E1 training-reward mean and SD. With the plain critic, M moves the
crossing by \VsevenMBPlain{} relative to B, most of A's \VsevenABPlain{}, and A$-$M is \VsevenAMPlain{}, an
interval that includes zero, so the pre-declared prediction that A exceeds M under both critics is not met (the
lower bound of the pre-declared percentile interval touched zero). On E7 alone, therefore, the A$-$S contrast under a raw-target critic
could be an effect of the reward's moments rather than of its sign asymmetry; E8 below tests this. With the PopArt critic M is
indistinguishable from B (\VsevenMBPop{}), while A exceeds both S (\VsevenAsymPop{}) and M (\VsevenAMPop{});
the change in A$-$M between critics is \VsevenAMChange{}. M's constants match A's moments under E1's policies, not
under M's own: its achieved SD matches A's, but its mean falls below A's at middle weights, failing the pre-declared
25\% matching check, so the plain-critic reading cannot separate a moment effect from that mean difference; with the
plain critic M's critic targets are also larger than A's (Appendix~\ref{app:moments}).



\paragraph{An online moment-matched control separates moments from shape (E8).}
E8's control M$_{\mathrm{on}}$ applies the same kind of increasing affine map to B's reward, but re-sets its
constants before every rollout. They are chosen so that its training-reward mean and SD track A's, rollout by
rollout, under M$_{\mathrm{on}}$'s own policy; the targets are the seed-averaged per-rollout moments of A in the
same critic mode, frozen before any E8 run. The matching criterion is met. Pooled over the eight cells, M$_{\mathrm{on}}$'s
achieved mean and SD lie within \VeightMatchMeanPlain{} and \VeightMatchSdPlain{} of A's with the plain critic,
and within \VeightMatchMeanPop{} and \VeightMatchSdPop{} with PopArt; \VeightClamp{} of \VeightNA{} runs
reached the declared lower bound on the scale at some rollout. Matching the mean leaves the control where it
was: M$_{\mathrm{on}}-$M is \VeightMonMPlain{} (plain) and \VeightMonMPop{} (PopArt), so E7's mean mismatch was
not driving its result. With PopArt, A$-$M$_{\mathrm{on}}$ is \VeightAMonPop{}. With the plain critic it is
\VeightAMonPlain{}, which excludes zero at 95\% but not at 99\% (\VeightAMonPlainNine{}), and the contrast
does not survive the Holm adjustment across the principal family (Section~\ref{sec:stats}). By the
pre-declared rule, which asks only for a 95\% interval that excludes zero, the plain-critic asymmetric contrast is
therefore not fully explained by the training reward's first two moments, and the E7 reading above is revised. It is
the weakest asymmetric result we report: small, and robust neither to a 99\% interval nor to the Holm adjustment.
M$_{\mathrm{on}}-$B is \VeightMonBPlain{}, most of A$-$B (\VsevenABPlain{}). So with a raw-target critic, most of
the loss-only shift is reproduced by a symmetric reward with the same moments; only the remainder,
A$-$M$_{\mathrm{on}}$, depends on the asymmetric shape. With PopArt the moment-matched symmetric control has no detectable effect
(\VeightMonBPop{}), and the asymmetric effect is large. Two features of the design limit this reading.
M$_{\mathrm{on}}$'s map changes between rollouts whereas A's is fixed. And an affine control matches only the first
two moments, so A$-$M$_{\mathrm{on}}$ contrasts A with a moment-matched, nonstationary symmetric control, not a
manipulation of sign asymmetry alone.

\section{A Second Environment: Level-Based Foraging}
\label{sec:lbf}

\paragraph{Design (E4).}
We repeated the arm comparison with centralized-critic MAPPO~\citep{yu2022surprising} on Level-Based
Foraging~\citep{christianos2020shared} (5$\times$5 grid, two players, two foods), using the same SB3
update with a global clip or with separate optimizers. The local reward is the native food share minus
an effort cost of 0.02 per LOAD attempt; the team reward sums native food rewards; collapse is a native
team return below $\theta=0.626$ over ten evaluation episodes, the midpoint between collapsed and coordinated team returns in an earlier calibration; the evaluation never includes the effort cost. The arms are
linear, curvature only, A ($\lambda=4$) and S (both branches $\times4$), on ten mixture cells with ten
seeds and 2M timesteps (800 trajectories). The transform is applied to each agent's blended reward with reference $z_0=0$ and unit
input scale, unlike the supply chain's fitted $z_0$. Food shares are therefore gains, and a step falls on the
loss branch only when its effort cost exceeds its food share.

\paragraph{The asymmetric contrast replicates under both optimizers.}
A raises the crossing relative to S by \VfourLBFAsymGlobal{} with the global clip and \VfourLBFAsymSep{}
with separate optimizers, and relative to curvature only by \VfourLBFLossOnlyGlobal{} and
\VfourLBFLossOnly{} (Table~\ref{tab:lbf}); the two optimizer modes do not differ detectably. The
asymmetric contrast holds for collapse thresholds from 0.5 to 0.75 and under the isotonic estimator
(Table~\ref{tab:lbf_theta}). Here, unlike the supply chain, the critic does not dominate the clip: the
actor carries \VfourLBFActorShare{} of the squared gradient norm in every arm. The loss branch is reached
almost only through the LOAD cost, so the loss multiplier changes the cost of the cooperative action
relative to food rewards, while S scales both and leaves their ratio unchanged; under A the share of
training rewards below zero falls to a few percent at low and middle $w$ (Table~\ref{tab:lbf_occupancy}),
because agents stop attempting loads. We therefore read the LBF result as a second environment in which an
asymmetric transform moves the crossing through an incentive change, not as an independent confirmation of
the supply-chain mechanism. Figure~\ref{fig:lbf} shows the observed proportions behind Table~\ref{tab:lbf}.
Several arms are far from the logistic shape, which is why Firth and isotonic crossings differ more here
than on the supply chain (by up to 0.161). Figure~\ref{fig:lbf_sensitivity} shows every arm's crossing
under both estimators and three label thresholds: A's crossing lies above S's in all twelve combinations,
whereas the linear arm's crossing under the global clip leaves the grid and the curvature arm's moves with
the threshold, so only the A$-$S comparison is stable to these choices. The LBF label is a different
quantity from the supply-chain label, so LBF and supply-chain crossings are compared only in sign and
ordering, never in magnitude.

\section{Robustness and Sensitivity}
\label{sec:robustness}

\paragraph{A well-defined boundary exists at fixed training budget.}
At 300k steps on the supply-chain PPO substrate, collapse has a clear, tightly-estimated boundary in
reward-weighting space (Figure~\ref{fig:boundary}): the midpoint is \Headline{}. Observed
proportions and Wilson intervals show the sampled support of the crossing.

\paragraph{``Collapse'' is an operational threshold crossing, not a confirmed attractor.}
We tested the continuous base-stock statistic $\bar S$ for a distinct dynamical regime with bootstrap
95\% CIs on Sarle's bimodality coefficient~\citep{pfister2013bimodality} and Hartigan's dip
test~\citep{hartigan1985dip} in each cell of the legacy curvature-only sweep ($\lambda=1$,
$\alpha{=}\beta{=}0.88$; $w\in\{.5,.65,.75,.85,.95\}$; $n=20$ seeds per cell).
Sarle's coefficient rises near the crossing (0.59 at $w{=}0.65$ and 0.57 at $w{=}0.75$, both above the
0.555 rule of thumb) but its CI straddles the threshold everywhere, and the dip test never rejects
unimodality ($p\ge0.30$). At $n=20$ the dip test, calibrated against the uniform least-favorable null,
has little power, so non-rejection is not evidence of unimodality; it only means the data do not
establish a second regime. We therefore use \emph{threshold-defined behavioral crossing} throughout.

\paragraph{The boundary is finite-horizon.}
In the legacy cross-sectional sweep the linear-reward boundary slides left with budget: 0.821, 0.666,
0.622 and 0.572 at 150k, 300k, 450k and 900k requested agent transitions
(Figure~\ref{fig:boundary}). These are separate finite-budget estimates and say nothing about
within-policy recovery.

\paragraph{Following the same policies to 3.6M (E3, E5).}
E3 follows 270 trajectories to 3.6M transitions under the default learner, so its contrasts include the scale
pathway and do not measure loss asymmetry. Its $\lambda=7$-minus-curvature difference is positive at every primary
budget but shrinks, from \VfourHorizonStart{} at 300k to \VfourHorizonEnd{} at 3.6M. No condition meets the
pre-declared stability criterion; many collapsed trajectories recover and some non-collapsed ones relapse. All
crossings are therefore budget-conditional (Appendix~\ref{app:horizon}). The decoupled cohort of Section~\ref{sec:asymmetric}
(E5) shows the same instability in the absolute crossings while A$-$S stays positive.


\paragraph{Local observations with agent identity (E12).}
The supply-chain policy elsewhere, except in X5, is a homogeneous shared controller: every node receives the full
state and no identifier. In E12 (480 runs) each node observes only its own retail demand, inventory and incoming order
pipeline, zero-padded, plus a one-hot node identifier, in training and in evaluation, under E1's default and
decoupled learners (Table~\ref{tab:e12}). A$-$S remains positive under both: \VtwelveAsymDefault{} (default) and
\VtwelveAsymDec{} (decoupled). The pre-declared prediction that the default learner's symmetric shift persists is
not met: S$-$B is \VtwelveSymDefault{}. Local observation lowers S's crossing from \VtwelveDefaultSFull{} to
\VtwelveDefaultS{}, while B's moves from \VtwelveDefaultBFull{} to \VtwelveDefaultB{}. The clip still attenuates S's
actor: its mean applied gradient is \VtwelveAppliedRatio{} of B's (a descriptive diagnostic, not pre-declared). So
the attenuation is present but no longer produces a detectable shift; we have no test of why. With the decoupled
learner S$-$B is \VtwelveSymDec{}, as predicted, but B and S collapse almost only at $w=0$. Their crossings
(\VtwelveDecB{} and \VtwelveDecS{}) therefore sit at the grid's lower edge, the interval is wide, and this verdict
carries little information.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Mechanism.} The mechanism we pre-registered, the shared clip starving the actor, is measured but not
shown to move the crossing: the per-group reduction held in one ten-seed cohort (E6) and not on fresh seeds (X1), and
neither group's clip has a detectable effect in the factorial (X3). The shift persists without any clipping, but an
exploratory pool of the two fresh cohorts suggests that clipping enlarges it (\VxeDiff{}), and the cohorts disagree on
this, so the clip's contribution is unresolved. In the CleanRL-style implementation a small per-group reduction
appears only when cohorts are pooled, a secondary analysis. Intervals that include zero do not
exclude a small clip effect; only the actor-clipping effect in X3 was tested for equivalence, and it lies within $\pm0.10$. The account that
remains, critic target scale, is tested directly in two pre-registered cohorts, X4 and, under decentralized
execution, X5: removing the target scale with a fixed-scale head, or normalizing the targets with PopArt, removes the
shift in each, on forty fresh seeds, consistent with the
normalization results of E1, E7, E8, E10 and E11. Neither identifies which property of the scaled critic carries it.
Both interventions equalize the critic's fit and the actor's advantage quality together with the target scale, so the
fit is a candidate mediator, not an isolated one, and the advantage measures depend on a reference critic that is
itself an estimate. X4 and X5 run at one budget, 300k, on the supply chain only. How much
of Adam's partial compensation under the shared clip is due to the varying clip factor and how much to $\epsilon$ is
not isolated.

\paragraph{Reward moments.} A and S differ in the training reward's mean and variance as well as in the
gain-to-loss ratio. The online control matches A's training-reward mean and SD but not its shape, and its map is
nonstationary where A's is fixed; \VeightClamp{} of its \VeightNA{} runs reached the declared bound on the scale.
With a raw-target critic, the part of the asymmetric effect not explained by the first two moments is small
(\VeightAMonPlain{}), and a moment-matched symmetric reward reproduces most of the loss-only shift.

\paragraph{Statistics.} Ten training seeds per cell give jackknife-$t$ contrast intervals of about $\pm0.06$--$0.15$ at
300k and up to $\pm0.19$ at 3.6M, and the pre-declared percentile intervals under-cover at this sample size
(Section~\ref{sec:stats}); \VpctSensN{} reported verdicts depend on which of the two is used (Table~\ref{tab:pct_sensitivity}).
Intervals that include zero are reported as no detectable effect, and none would support equivalence at a
$\pm0.05$ margin. The 24-checkpoint E5 reading is exploratory and its intervals are pointwise.

\paragraph{Information structure and generality.} Apart from E12 and X5 the supply-chain policy is a fully observed shared
controller without agent identity (Section~\ref{sec:method}). Under local observation with an identifier (E12) the
asymmetric contrast holds, but the default learner's symmetric shift is no longer detectable, contrary to our
prediction, and we have not explained why. With per-node actors on local views and a centralized critic (X5) the
shift is present and, at this budget and without clipping, has the same channel. The supply-chain symmetric shift is therefore established for the shared
controller and for per-node actors with a centralized critic, each at 300k; with a parameter-shared critic on local
views (E12, ten seeds) it was not detected, which is inconclusive. In LBF, symmetric $\times4$ scaling moves the crossing by \VfourLBFSymGlobal{} under the global
clip and not detectably without it (\VfourLBFSymSep{}); with LBF's actor-dominated gradient, this is not evidence for the
supply-chain mechanism. Two small environments and two PPO implementations are tested: SB3 2.8.0 and a CleanRL-style port
run in the same environment and harness (E11). Other implementations, clipping conventions and value normalizations
may couple actor and critic differently, and LBF does not exhibit the supply chain's critic-dominated joint gradient norm. In LBF the linear arm's crossing under the global clip lies below the sampled
grid, so contrasts with it are not identified. No condition has a stable crossing by 3.6M, so all readings are
finite-budget. An earlier ablation on the supply chain found no interior crossing for PPO, QMIX or IQL on a
coarse 11-level discretization of the base-stock action. Crossings are conditional on the behavioral label and
the sampled grid; non-bracketed estimates are reported, not clipped.

\paragraph{Open tests.} Four gaps remain, and the paper's claims stop short of each.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Critics that see only local information.} X5's critic is centralized, and E12, whose critic was local but
parameter-shared, was inconclusive.
\item \emph{The clipped shift under decentralized execution.} X5 ran without clipping. It does not show that the larger
default shift transfers to per-node actors, or how much of that shift runs through the critic's targets.
\item \emph{Fit as the mediator.} The critic's fit is not separated from its target scale.
\item \emph{A second environment.} The only other environment tested, LBF, has an actor-dominated gradient, so the
critic-target shift is shown on the supply chain alone.
\end{itemize}
Three further experiments address the first three gaps: X6 (per-node local critics), X7 (X5's architecture under the
shared clip, with critics whose targets and gradients are of the baseline's size, and per-group clipping) and X8 (a
critic slowed to the scaled arm's fit at the baseline's target scale). They were pre-registered and frozen before any
run (Appendix~\ref{app:prereg}); their results are not part of this version.

\paragraph{Pre-registration record.} E4's stored labels used a fallback threshold of 0.5 instead of the
declared 0.626 because a calibration file was missing from the frozen source; all E4 results are recomputed
from the stored team returns (deviation D4). Each stored team return equals the mean of the run's ten stored
per-episode returns (largest difference $\VdaEMaxDiff{}$, all 800 runs), and the stored labels are exactly the
0.5-threshold labels. Exactly \VdaETies{} run has a return of 0.75, so its label in the $\theta=0.75$ sensitivity
analysis depends on floating-point summation order; no other threshold is affected. The provenance of 0.626 is an earlier calibration whose raw
records are not archived, so the threshold itself is taken as declared, not re-derived; Figure~\ref{fig:lbf_sensitivity}
shows that the A$-$S ordering does not depend on it.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What is established.}
Three findings are supported at the level of evidence stated in Table~\ref{tab:hierarchy} and
Sections~\ref{sec:asymmetric} and~\ref{sec:lbf}. First, a finite-budget crossing along the reward-weighting
dial can be estimated with seed-level jackknife-$t$ intervals of roughly $\pm0.04$--$0.13$, but it moves with training
budget, does not stabilize by 3.6M transitions, and depends on the optimizer configuration. Second, under
SB3's default PPO a symmetric reward multiplier moves the crossing at least as far as a loss-only multiplier, and
the same confound appears in a second, CleanRL-style implementation. With normalized rewards no symmetric shift is
detectable in either implementation. Without clipping a smaller shift remains, and pre-registered tests on fresh
seeds, at this budget, show that fixing the scale of the critic's regression targets or normalizing them removes it, both with a shared controller (X4) and with per-node actors on local observations and a
centralized critic (X5). Both interventions also equalize the critic's fit, so whether the fit is the mediator is not
isolated. The mechanism we pre-registered is not supported: the shared gradient clip measurably starves the actor's
update, but on fresh seeds restoring the actor's step does not reduce the shift, and the shift persists without any
clipping in SB3 (E1, X3), though it was not detectable in X1; clipping may enlarge it. The CleanRL-style port was not
run without clipping. With a shared actor and critic both on local views (E12) the symmetric shift was not detectable,
which is inconclusive, and in LBF it is small.
Third, with separate optimizers and
normalized rewards, a loss-only multiplier moves the crossing relative to a symmetric control on the
supply chain, through 3.6M transitions, and an asymmetric transform moves it on Level-Based Foraging by a
different route. Against a symmetric control matched online in mean and SD (E8), the supply-chain effect is
large when the critic's targets are normalized and small when they are not (detectable at 95\%, but not at 99\% or after a
Holm adjustment). With a raw-target critic, therefore, most of the loss-only shift is reproduced by a symmetric
reward with the same mean and SD, and the shape-specific part is the weakest result we report. None of this is evidence
about loss aversion as a psychological mechanism, nor about loss-asymmetric rewards in general: the effect is
a property of the transformed training signal, and in LBF it is plainly an incentive change on a costly
cooperative action.

\paragraph{Implications for reward-transform experiments.}
The practical lesson is about design, not about loss asymmetry. A reward-transform comparison in
actor--critic MARL should include a symmetric-scaling control at the same multiplier and should report
whether actor and critic share a gradient clip and whether rewards or value targets are normalized; without
these, a change in reward scale can pass for a change in reward shape.

\paragraph{Implications for actor--critic optimization.}
The confound needs an unnormalized critic, which is SB3 PPO's default. Advantages are normalized per minibatch,
so the actor's loss is nearly scale-free, yet the crossing still moves with the reward scale, and the critic fits
the scaled returns worse in every clipping mode we tested; fixing the scale of its targets removes the shift (X4, and X5 under decentralized execution). CleanRL's default PPO normalizes rewards, and in our
port that default leaves no detectable symmetric shift (E11); without it, the same port reproduces the confound.
Cooperative PPO implementations that normalize value targets by default~\citep{yu2022surprising} should be less
exposed; we did not test them. A methodological lesson concerns the evidence for a mechanism: a large, directly
measured optimizer distortion (the actor's Adam step at \VxoGlobStep{} of baseline) and an intervention in one
cohort both pointed to the clip, and only pre-registered replications on fresh seeds showed that undoing the distortion
does not undo the behavioral effect.

\paragraph{Interpreting finite-budget crossings.}
$\wcrit$ is a 50\% point of a fitted response curve at a stated budget, learner, reward unit and label. It is
not a critical point, a safe operating point or an asymptotic property. Trajectories both recover from
and relapse into collapse (Table~\ref{tab:horizon_stability}), so the crossing summarizes a population of
training outcomes, not an attractor. Contrasts between arms within one configuration are more stable than
absolute crossings: in E5 the absolute crossings fall and rise again, while A$-$S stays positive. A
deployment requirement such as at most 5\% collapse would need its own probability target and uncertainty
assessment.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.}
All E1--E12 and X1--X5 runs share one platform; trained policies are not bit-reproducible across hardware and software
builds, but labels agree in \VfourAgreeControl{} of matched runs, so we claim reproducibility of labels and
crossings, not of policies. The artifact rebuilds every reported table, figure and number from per-run
records with one command (Appendix~\ref{app:artifact}).

\paragraph{Conclusion.}
Reward centralization is usually treated as a direction: more team signal helps. It also has a measurable
finite-budget location, $\wcrit$, that moves with reward transforms, training budget and the optimizer.
Measured with default SB3 PPO, a symmetric reward multiplier and a loss-only multiplier cannot be told apart, and a
CleanRL-style PPO without reward normalization shows the same symmetric shift. With normalized rewards no symmetric shift is
detectable. Without clipping, at 300k, a smaller shift remains, and it is carried by the scale of the critic's
regression targets: fixing that scale, or normalizing the targets, removes it, with a shared controller and with
decentralized actors, whether or not through the critic's fit. The shared gradient clip, the obvious route by which reward scale reaches the actor's
updates, is not shown to be needed, and restoring the actor's step does not reduce the shift. In SB3 on the supply chain, with
clipping removed and rewards normalized, the loss-only multiplier moves the crossing and the symmetric one produces
no detectable shift; the CleanRL-style port with normalized rewards and its shared clip also shows no detectable
symmetric shift and a positive A$-$S, and an asymmetric transform moves the crossing in Level-Based Foraging. How much of that shift is about sign
asymmetry rather than the reward's moments depends on the critic. With raw value targets, a symmetric control with
matched mean and SD reproduces most of it, leaving a small asymmetric remainder; with normalized value targets it
reproduces almost none. Reward-transform comparisons in
actor--critic MARL should therefore include symmetric-scaling controls and report the reward or value
normalization, gradient clipping and training budget under which they were run.
